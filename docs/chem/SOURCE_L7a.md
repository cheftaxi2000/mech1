### Slide 5 (p. 4) — Today: Basics of Chemical Bonding
- Handwritten «We have mentioned several times …» → «Atoms or ions are held together in molecules and solids by bonds».

### Slide 6 (p. 5) — Lewis Symbols
- Handwritten top: «To discuss bonding, we first introduce a simple tool …»
- «For each atom: draw atomic symbol plus v.e.'s (as "dots")».
- Ex: sulfur [Ne] 3s²3p⁴ ⇒ 6 valence electrons, drawn two ways (handwritten «Both are correct»).
- Rules: place 2 "dots" maximum on each of 4 sides; spread out the valence electrons available; top, bottom, left, and right sides of symbol are all equivalent.
- Other ex's: periodic-table cells Li 2s¹, Be 2s², B 2s²2p¹, C 2s²2p², N 2s²2p³, O 2s²2p⁴, F 2s²2p⁵, Ne 2s²2p⁶ ([He] core) with their Lewis symbols.

### Slide 7 (p. 6) — Octet Rule
«Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons until surrounded by 8 v.e.'s». Why 8? Because then they have the stable "noble gas" configuration (Ne cell and :Ne:). Note: exceptions exist to the octet rule; still very useful idea to understand bonding; most useful for atoms with v.e.'s in s and p subshells («rather than d and f subshells»).

### Slide 8 (p. 7) — Ionic Bonding
- Recall (margin): periodic table with metals (left) and non-metals (right).
- Metal atoms lose valence electrons, non-metal atoms gain valence electrons } to satisfy the octet rule.
- Ex: NaCl: Na· + ·Cl: (arrow: the electron goes from Na to Cl) → Na⁺ + [:Cl:]⁻ → Na⁺Cl⁻(s). Margin: «In this process: Na goes to [Ne], Cl goes to [Ar]».
- Ionic bonding is strong! Due to electrostatic attraction of ions. «Opposite charges attract! This lowers energy as they approach.»
- Strength quantified by **lattice energy**: «Energy required to separate ions to infinite distance».
- Ex: NaCl: Na⁺Cl⁻(s) → Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g), ΔH_lat = +788 kJ/mol. «How can we calculate the lattice energy?»
- Margin: «Coulombic attraction of opposite charges (Exercise #1)», E_el = (1/4πε₀)·Q₁Q₂/d; «Ions want to be close together (i.e., small d) to lower their energy.»
- Bottom handwritten: «∴ The reverse reaction, forming NaCl(s) from Na⁺ and Cl⁻, is highly exothermic ⇒ −ΔH_lat».

### Slide 9 (p. 8) — Calculating the Lattice Energy
- Handwritten top: «We can calculate ΔH_lat by putting many ideas that we have learned together! ⇒ Hess's law, ΔH°_f, ionization energy, and electron affinity (EA)».
- Diagram (energy upward): NaCl(s) → (−ΔH°f[NaCl(s)]) Na(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → (ΔH°f[Na(g)]) Na(g) + ½Cl₂(g) → (ΔH°f[Cl(g)]) Na(g) + Cl(g) → (I₁(Na)) Na⁺(g) + e⁻ + Cl(g) → (EA(Cl), down) Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g); red arrow «Lattice energy of NaCl» from NaCl(s) up to Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g). ΔH_lat = +788 kJ/mol.
- Bottom: «See p. 376 of Brown for details». **No numerical values of the individual steps on the slide.**

### Slide 10 (p. 9) — Covalent Bonding
- Handwritten top: «But most substances are not ionic (e.g., molecules are not ionic). They exploit …»
- Two atoms share their valence electrons ⇒ covalent bond. The shared pair of valence electrons ⇒ "glue" that holds atoms together.
- Ex: H₂: H· + ·H → H:H (grey overlapping circles); Cl₂: :Cl· + ·Cl: → :Cl:Cl: } «Each atom now has noble-gas configuration ⇒ He and Ar, respectively». Margin: «Lewis "dot" structures».
- Bonding pair drawn as line: H–H, :Cl–Cl: } unshared or non-bonding electrons kept as dots ⇒ "lone pairs". Handwritten arrow: «the "bond"».
- «The atoms can also share multiple pairs …»

### Slide 11 (p. 10) — Multiple Covalent Bonds
Single pair of shared electrons represents one covalent bond ⇒ single bond. Two atoms that share two pairs ⇒ double bond. Ex: CO₂: :O: + ·C· + :O: → O::C::O or O=C=O. Three pairs ⇒ triple bond. Ex: N₂: ·N: + ·N: → :N:::N: or :N≡N:. «In general, bond lengths decrease with number of shared electron pairs». Ex: N–N 147, N=N 124, N≡N 110 pm.

### Slide 12 (p. 11) — How Are Valence Electrons Shared in Covalent Bond?
- Handwritten top: «But Lewis structures imply that electron pair is shared equally between the bonded atoms.»
- Bond polarity: how equally is bonding pair distributed? If equally ⇒ nonpolar covalent bond (Ex: Cl₂, N₂; margin «The bonded atoms are the same. Thus, it makes sense that the electrons are shared equally!»). Unequally ⇒ polar covalent bond (Ex: HF).
- To estimate polarity of bond, we can use … **Electronegativity**: ability of atom in molecule to attract electrons to itself. Electronegative atoms: large negative electron affinity, large ionization energy (handwritten: «Atoms that attract electrons (EA ≪ 0) and resist giving electrons away (I₁ ≫ 0)»).
- Bottom handwritten: «Electronegativity scale (Linus Pauling, 1901–1994) based on thermodynamic data [no units].»

### Slide 15 (p. 14) — Calculate Bond Polarity?
Use electronegativity difference. Table! (margin «Given at exam! (It's on "useful info sheet")»). Ex: HF 4.0 − 2.1 = 1.9 ⇒ polar covalent bond. Can represent with **partial charges**: δ⁺ H–F δ⁻ (δ⁺ red «partial positive charge», δ⁻ blue «partial negative charge»; margin «Like with H₂O. See Lecture #3 p. 8»). Rule of thumb: if electronegativity difference ≥ 2 ⇒ ionic bond. Electronegativity increases as we move right and to the top of periodic table. Handwritten: «Ex: LiF difference is 4.0 − 1.0 = 3.0 ⇒ ionic».

### Slide 16 (p. 15) — Dipole Moments
Partial charges lead to **dipole moment**, μ⃗ (margin «μ ≡ "mu"»). δ⁺ H–F δ⁻ with μ⃗ drawn under the bond pointing from F to H. «Separation of charge quantified by vector pointing from negative to positive». |μ⃗| = Qr (margin «Magnitude of dipole moment»); ±Q ≡ charges separated by distance r (margin «For HF, Q = δ»). Very polar bonds ⇒ large |μ⃗|. Units? Debye (D), D = 3.34 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m (margin «Coulomb-meter»). A proton and electron separated by 0.1 nm ⇒ |μ⃗| = 4.8 D. |μ⃗_HF| = 1.8 D. Bottom: «Note: We are using the physicist's convention for the direction of the dipole (negative to positive). Chemists use opposite convention.» Margin: «Typical dipole moments for diatomic molecules ⇒ 0–10 D».

### Slide 17 (p. 16) — Ionic versus Covalent
Not a strict line between ⇒ continuum of behavior. More covalent ⇒ molecular behavior ⇒ low melting/boiling temperature (margin «Also, forms nonelectrolytes in H₂O»). More ionic ⇒ ionic solids ⇒ brittle, high-melting temperature. Electronegativity difference rule of thumb ⇒ useful, but not perfect (margin «In other words, the rule that differences above 2 are ionic bonds is not perfect»). As oxidation number of metal increases ⇒ bonding more covalent. Ex: MnO (+2 −2) IONIC; Mn₂O₇ (+7 −2) COVALENT. Handwritten: «Manganese(II) oxide. Forms an ionic solid with melting temperature of 1842 °C.» «Manganese(VII) oxide. Forms a liquid at 25 °C. Molecular behavior due to covalent bonding.» «This change is not apparent if we just look at electronegativity difference (2.1)» — see CH-013.
