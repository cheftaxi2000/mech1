## Lecture 1

**10 (p. 9) What is Chemistry?** «Study of properties and behavior of matter» (marked *This is only one possible definition* *(hw)*; *Matter: what the universe is made of (has mass and volume)* *(hw)*). «Language of Chemistry? How do we describe matter?» Letters? **Atoms** = smallest unit of an element; **Elements** = substances made of only one atom type (e.g. carbon, C; hydrogen, H₂ *(hw)*). Words? **Molecules** = two or more atoms joined together; **Compounds** = molecules with different types of atoms (e.g. water, H₂O *(hw)*). Sentences? **Reactions** = describe «chemical changes» (*Changing from one chemical (substance) to another chemical (substance)* *(hw)*). *(hw)* e.g. = exempli gratia = «for example».

**11 (p. 10) Pure Substances versus Mixtures.** Pure substances: examples elements, molecules, compounds; fixed composition; composition does not change from sample to sample. Mixtures: contain two or more pure substances; if uniform → homogeneous mixture; if nonuniform → heterogeneous mixture. Examples: pure water is a pure substance; mineral water is a homogeneous mixture; milk is a heterogeneous mixture (photo of a glass of milk). *(hw)* top: «Elements, molecules, and compounds are "pure substances". They have distinct properties and compositions that do not change from one sample to another. We can distinguish pure substances from mixtures …» Side: «In a mixture, each component keeps its own chemical identity and properties. Mixtures can be separated into pure substances.» Bottom: «"Uniform" means composition, properties, appearance is the same everywhere in mixture; "Nonuniform" means … vary across sample of the mixture»; «Mineral water = solution (more later)»; «Earth's atmosphere is heterogeneous mixture: its composition changes with altitude».

**12 (p. 11) Atoms — Dalton's Atomic Theory (1803–1807)** (portrait John Dalton, 1766–1844). «We now accept that matter is made from atoms, but not obvious!» Bullets: each element composed of atoms; all atoms of a given element are chemically identical; different elements composed of different atoms; atoms cannot be changed, created, or destroyed by chemical reactions; compounds involve combinations of atoms of 2 or more elements. «Explains Laws of … Constant Composition, Conservation of Mass, Multiple Proportions.» *(hw)* Constant Composition: in a given compound, number and type of atoms is fixed. Conservation of Mass: mass does not change due to chemical reactions. Law of Multiple Proportions: if 2 elements combine to form several different compounds, their masses combine in ratios of small whole numbers.

**13 (p. 12) Atomic Structure** (portrait Marie Curie). «We now know atoms are made from even smaller subatomic particles …» Electrons, protons, neutrons — table: electrons q_e = −e, m_e = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg; protons q_p = +e, m_p = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg; neutrons q_n = 0, m_n = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg; e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C. «Charged» brace on electrons and protons. Notes: atoms have equal number of protons and electrons *(hw: to be electrically neutral)*; protons and neutrons much heavier than electrons *(hw: 1836× and 1839×, respectively)*; these masses are tiny → inconvenient in kg *(hw: we will fix this shortly (with amu))*.

**14 (p. 13) The Modern Picture of the Atom.** Figure: red blurred electron cloud with a dot. Nucleus (protons and neutrons) ~10⁻⁴ Å; 1 Å = 1×10⁻¹⁰ m; electron cloud 1–5 Å. «Nucleus incredibly dense! If matchbox had same density (10¹⁴ g/cm³) would weigh 2.6 billion tons!» Photo of a football stadium: «Cartoon nucleus not to scale! If electron cloud were football pitch, nucleus would be 1 mm in diameter.»

**15 (p. 14) What Makes One Type of Atom Different from Another?** «Each type of atom has specific number of protons, given by its …» **Atomic number** = number of protons in atoms of particular element; also gives number of electrons (to keep charge neutrality). Ex's: carbon atoms have 6 protons, 6 electrons → atomic number 6; oxygen atoms have 8 protons, 8 electrons → atomic number 8 *(hw: all carbon atoms in the universe have 6 protons and 6 electrons; same for oxygen with 8)*. Notes: «Chemical properties of atom determined by its electrons!» «Atomic number gives number of protons or electrons in an atom.» «What about neutrons?»

**16 (p. 15) Neutrons.** «For specific element, number of neutrons in its atoms can vary …» **Isotopes** = same element with different number of neutrons; affects mass of atom, but not its chemistry. **Mass number** = number of protons plus neutrons in atom. Ex: carbon: most common isotope has 6 neutrons → mass number 12 *(hw: most common carbon isotope «carbon-12»; 98.93 % of all carbon has 6 neutrons)*. **Atomic symbols**: box with ¹²₆C: mass number upper left, atomic number lower left, «Atomic symbol». «Atomic number is redundant once symbol and mass number are given.» *(hw)* Note: ¹²C is a stable isotope, but many isotopes are unstable and «decay» by releasing sub-atomic particles and energy → radioactivity. Ex: uranium ²³⁸U 99.28 %, ²³⁵U 0.71 %; small drawing of ²³⁵U + n → fission into ¹⁴¹Ba, ⁹²Kr and 3 neutrons.

**17 (p. 16) Atomic Mass and Atomic Weight.** «To avoid using kg's and extremely small numbers, we define a new mass scale for atoms …» **Atomic mass** = new scale with ¹²C mass equal to 12 u; 1 u = 1.660539040×10⁻²⁷ kg (atomic mass unit, amu). Masses: m_p = 1.0073 u, m_n = 1.0087 u, m_e = 5.486×10⁻⁴ u. **Atomic weight** = average atomic mass of element in amu's; use relative abundance of each isotope of the specific element. Ex carbon: ¹²C 98.93 %, ¹³C 1.07 %, ¹⁴C trace. Atomic weight of C = (0.9893·12 u) + (0.0107·13.0035 u) + trace = 12.01 u.

**18 (p. 17) The Periodic Table.** *(hw)* «Atoms organized into a table (Dmitri Mendeleev, 1869) …» Table of 118 elements, enlarged cell for K: 19 (atomic number), K (atomic symbol), 39.0983 (atomic weight). Bullets: lists 118 known elements; in order of atomic number; gives symbol and atomic weight; columns known as «groups»; in group: chemically similar behavior; rows known as periods; quantum mechanics explains shape; tan = metals; green = non-metals; purple = metalloids. *(hw)* «The "alphabet" of chemistry. All the elements in the universe. Groups 1–18. Black line divides metallic and non-metallic behavior. Metalloids have properties in between metals and non-metals.» In the figure the metalloids are B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te; At is a non-metal, Po a metal; the f-block is drawn below (57–70, 89–102).

**19 (p. 18) Common Names.** Periodic table with groups 3–12 braced as «Transition metals», the two f-block rows as «Lanthanides» (hw: rare earths) and «Actinides». Table: group 1 alkali metals Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr; group 2 alkaline earth metals Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra; group 16 chalcogens O, S, Se, Te, Po; group 17 halogens F, Cl, Br, I, At; group 18 noble gases He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn. *(hw)* Lutetium, Lu (71) is included in lanthanides; Lawrencium, Lr (103) is included in actinides.

**20 (p. 19) Molecules and Compounds.** *(hw)* «We will discuss ions shortly.» Bullets: only noble gases exist as isolated atoms; most matter exists as molecules or ions; represent with chemical formulas. Pictures (space-filling): hydrogen H₂, oxygen O₂, carbon dioxide CO₂, water H₂O, hydrogen peroxide H₂O₂, methane CH₄. «Represent connectivity and shape?» Methane as **structural formula** (H–C–H with H above and below), **perspective drawing** (dashed wedge = bond behind page; solid line = bond in plane of page; wedge = bond out of page), **ball-and-stick model** («Even better»), **space-filling model** («Best»). *(hw)* Structural formula is nice and simple: easy to draw and shows connectivity, but not 3D shape. Perspective drawing can be drawn and shows 3D shape. «Ball and stick» shows bond angles; space-filling also captures volume of electron clouds (but both difficult to draw).

**21 (p. 20) Ions.** Ions are electrically charged atoms or molecules; cations have positive charge, i.e. electron(s) removed; anions have negative charge, i.e. electron(s) added; atoms at edges of the periodic table tend to match number of e⁻'s in noble gas (small periodic table with typical ions: H⁺; Li⁺ Na⁺ K⁺ Rb⁺ Cs⁺; Mg²⁺ Ca²⁺ Sr²⁺ Ba²⁺; Al³⁺; N³⁻ O²⁻ F⁻ S²⁻ Cl⁻ Se²⁻ Br⁻ Te²⁻ I⁻; H⁻). Picture: Na atom 11p⁺, 11e⁻ → loses an electron → Na cation (1+) 11p⁺, 10e⁻ (smaller); Cl atom 17p⁺, 17e⁻ → gains an electron → Cl anion (1−) 17p⁺, 18e⁻ (larger). «Note: atom's size changes when ion forms» (red). i.e. = id est = «that is»; e⁻'s = «electrons». *(hw)* Convention (as in the textbook): ionic charges are given in multiples of e with the sign after the number, e.g. Na¹⁺ and Cl¹⁻ (sic, «Na¹⁺ and Cl¹⁻»; in the lecture the charge of a single-charge ion is written «+» / «−»).

**22 (p. 21) Polyatomic Ions / Ionic Compounds.** Polyatomic ions: ions made from a group of atoms, e.g. ammonium cation NH₄⁺, sulfate anion SO₄²⁻. Ionic compounds: formed between cation and anion; generally between metal and nonmetal; example NaCl (table salt); electrons transferred from metal to non-metal; oppositely charged ions attract; compounds are electrically neutral; if charges are unequal on cation and anion, balance charge with coefficients: Mg²⁺ and N³⁻ → Mg₃N₂ (arrows cross the charges: the 2 becomes the subscript of N, the 3 the subscript of Mg). Picture of the NaCl solid (cubic lattice of purple Na⁺ and green Cl⁻). «Please read Brown 2.8: Naming Inorganic Compounds.» *(hw)* For an ionic solid, we write its empirical formula.

**23 (p. 22) Common Cations** (table from Brown; bold = most used in the course, «Learn them first»). 1+: H⁺ hydrogen, Li⁺ lithium, **Na⁺** sodium, **K⁺** potassium, Cs⁺ cesium, **Ag⁺** silver; **NH₄⁺** ammonium, Cu⁺ copper(I)/cuprous. 2+: **Mg²⁺** magnesium, **Ca²⁺** calcium, Sr²⁺ strontium, Ba²⁺ barium, **Zn²⁺** zinc, Cd²⁺ cadmium; Co²⁺ cobalt(II), **Cu²⁺** copper(II)/cupric, **Fe²⁺** iron(II)/ferrous, Mn²⁺ manganese(II), Hg₂²⁺ mercury(I)/mercurous, Hg²⁺ mercury(II)/mercuric, Ni²⁺ nickel(II), **Pb²⁺** lead(II)/plumbous, Sn²⁺ tin(II)/stannous. 3+: **Al³⁺** aluminium, Cr³⁺ chromium(III), **Fe³⁺** iron(III)/ferric. (In the figure H⁺ and Na⁺, K⁺, Ag⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Pb²⁺, Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, NH₄⁺ are bold.)

**24 (p. 23) Common Anions.** 1−: H⁻ hydride, **F⁻** fluoride, **Cl⁻** chloride, **Br⁻** bromide, **I⁻** iodide, CN⁻ cyanide, **OH⁻** hydroxide; **CH₃COO⁻** (or C₂H₃O₂⁻) acetate, ClO₃⁻ chlorate, **ClO₄⁻** perchlorate, **NO₃⁻** nitrate, MnO₄⁻ permanganate. 2−: **O²⁻** oxide, O₂²⁻ peroxide, **S²⁻** sulfide; **CO₃²⁻** carbonate, CrO₄²⁻ chromate, Cr₂O₇²⁻ dichromate, **SO₄²⁻** sulfate. 3−: N³⁻ nitride, **PO₄³⁻** phosphate. Footnote: the ions we use most often are in boldface; learn them first.

**25 (p. 24) Chemical Reactions.** *(hw)* «Now we have "letters" and "words". What about "sentences"?» Describe changes from one substance to another; distinct from physical changes of the same substance (ex: ice melting is not a chemical reaction; *(hw)* physical changes are changes in appearance (solid, liquid, vapor)). Rules of chemical reactions: atoms are neither created nor destroyed during reaction; energy may be required to start reaction; energy may be released or absorbed by reaction (*(hw)* energy is one reason chemical reactions are so important to mechanical and process engineers; chemical reactions power the world, and this will not change in a carbon-neutral future). Described by chemical equations: reactants → products («go to», «produce»); each atom type must be balanced on both «sides» (*(hw)* because atoms cannot be created or destroyed during the reaction).

**26 (p. 25) Stoichiometry.** «How many reactants are consumed and products produced.» Ex: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O (reactant #1, reactant #2, product; releases useful energy; important reaction for future of our planet! *(hw)* H₂-powered turbines for electricity production). «Not balanced! 2H + 2O ≠ 2H + O — just counting atoms.» Balancing chemical equations: either by «inspection» … 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O («Often one can see coefficients needed to balance»; *(hw)* balance by placing coefficients in front of reactants and products, like an algebraic equation) … or systematically →.

**27 (p. 26) Balancing Chemical Equations.** *(hw)* «Balancing an equation is a little puzzle to solve …» Systematic approach: a H₂ + b O₂ → c H₂O (a, b, c are unknown coefficients). Balance requires a = c and b = ½c. Set a = 1 ⇒ c = 1 and b = ½: H₂ + ½ O₂ → H₂O. Or multiply all coefficients by 2 …: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O (*(hw)* to have whole-number coefficients). «Both balanced. You can always check!» **Note:** to balance equation, change coefficients, not subscripts! H₂O ≠ H₂O₂. *(hw)* «This was a simple example, but reactions can be more complicated (as we will see).»

**28 (p. 27) Formula Weight.** *(hw)* «In real life, we don't typically have two H₂ molecules reacting with one O₂ molecule. Rather, we need to discuss macroscopic quantities, like grams or kilograms.» «We need to know mass of substances.» Formula weight (FW) is sum of atomic weights for all atoms in formula. Ex: H₂O: FW of H₂O = 2·(1.0 u) + 1·(16.0 u) = 18.0 u (atomic weight of hydrogen, atomic weight of oxygen, mass of one H₂O molecule in atomic mass units). If substance is a molecule ⇒ FW is called **molecular weight, M_w** (*(hw)* M_w is symbol for molecular weight); ionic solid ⇒ for FW use **empirical formula** (gives relative amount of atoms in compound in smallest whole-number ratio), ex: NaCl, CaCl₂. *(hw)* Formula weights are the mass of a single molecule in atomic mass units. Remember (p. 21): solid NaCl is 3D arrangement of «NaCl» pairs.

**29 (p. 28) Sample Problem.** *(hw)* «Let's do a problem: if we have 2 g of H₂, how many grams of O₂ do we need for all H₂ to react? Formula weight gives us mass of molecule in amu's. So we first need to convert units. We need to know how many molecules …» *(hw)* Exercise #1 this week will have a tutorial on dimensional analysis. You will do such unit conversions a lot in your courses! Question: «How many grams of O₂ can react with 2.00 g of H₂?» Reaction 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O; FW of H₂ = 2·(1.00 u) = 2.00 u; FW of O₂ = 2·(16.0 u) = 32.0 u. Strategy: convert units: grams H₂ → amu's H₂ → molecules H₂ → molecules O₂ → amu's O₂ → grams O₂. Chain: 2 g H₂ · (1 kg / 1000 g) · (1 u / 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg) · (1 molecule H₂ / 2.00 u) · (1/2 molecule O₂ / 1 molecule H₂) · (32.0 u / 1 molecule O₂) · (1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg / 1 u) · (1000 g / 1 kg); intermediate results 6.02×10²³ molecules H₂ and 3.01×10²³ molecules O₂; **Answer = 16.0 g O₂**. *(hw)* Lessons: A. The answer is not 1.0 g O₂! Because 2 molecules of H₂ react with 1 molecule of O₂, and different molecules have different masses. B. This process is tedious! Is there an easier way?

**30 (p. 29) The Magnificent Mole.** 6.022140857×10²³ ≡ N_A ≡ Avogadro's number. «Why this number? For convenience!» Defined such that the atomic weight of any element in grams contains N_A atoms. From periodic table: potassium K, 19, 39.0983: one K atom has mass of 39.0983 u; 39.0983 g of K contains N_A K atoms; known as 1 mole of K. Also, molecular weight of any molecule in g's contains N_A of such molecules. Formula weight then gives **molar mass**: grams / mole — «Useful!» *(hw)* The mole is a huge quantity, which is hard to imagine! N_A marbles would create a 5 km-thick layer on Earth! (*(hw)* «Sorry for a bad pun! The Bahnhofstrasse of Chicago (Michigan Avenue) is known as "The Magnificent Mile."»; «This was a choice we made»; «We can then read it directly from periodic table»; «Allows us to convert easily between number and mass of atoms or molecules»; abbreviation for mole or moles ⇒ «mol»; note: we can have a mole of anything — it's a defined quantity like a dozen ≡ 12, a gross ≡ 12×12 = 144, a mole ≡ 6.02×10²³.) *(hw top)* «It would be easier to define a macroscopic number of atoms/molecules and then know how much mass in this standard number for every substance. This leads to definition of the …»

**31 (p. 30) Return to Stoichiometry.** *(hw)* «Let's return to stoichiometry …» Table: chemical equation 2 H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2 H₂O(l); molecular interpretation: 2 molecules H₂, 1 molecule O₂, 2 molecules H₂O (space-filling pictures); mole-level interpretation: 2 mol H₂, 1 mol O₂, 2 mol H₂O; «Convert to grams (using molar masses)» → 4.0 g H₂, 32.0 g O₂, 36.0 g H₂O. «1 mole is 6.02×10²³ "items" — Avogadro's number, N_A.» «Molar mass can be determined from formula weight.» *(hw)* Where we have now specified the state of each reactant and product, i.e. solid = s, liquid = l, or gas = g. Note: mass is conserved, as it must be according to the Law of Conservation of Mass.
