**17 (p. 15) Acids.** *(hw)* «Now we will introduce important class of aqueous reactions — even for mechanical engineers: acid/base chemistry. Many engineers intimidated by this topic. Don't need to be! Today introduce acid/base. We will return to it in Lecture 13.» **Acids = substances that ionize in H₂O to form hydrogen cations.** Common acids: HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) hydrochloric acid; HNO₃(aq) → H⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) nitric acid — both **monoprotic acids** *(hw: gives one H⁺ per molecule)*; H₂SO₄(aq) → H⁺(aq) + HSO₄⁻(aq) sulfuric acid; HSO₄⁻(aq) → H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) — together **diprotic acid** *(hw: gives two H⁺ per molecule)*. *(hw)* H⁺ also referred to as protons because that is what H⁺ is. Acids also known as «proton donors». *(hw)* Harder to release the second H⁺, so this arrow should actually be ⇌ (bidirectional) because this reaction is in equilibrium. This will make more sense after Lectures 12 and 13.

**18 (p. 16) Bases.** **Substances that accept (react with) hydrogen cations.** Bases produce hydroxide ions OH⁻ when dissolved in H₂O. Common bases: NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) sodium hydroxide; Ca(OH)₂(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) calcium hydroxide; NH₃(aq) + H₂O(ℓ) → NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) ammonia. *(hw)* Hydroxides are thus among the most common bases. But doesn't have to be a hydroxide to be a base. Common example: ammonia, NH₃.

**19 (p. 17) Strength of Acids and Bases.** Depends on how much H⁺ and OH⁻ produced, respectively. Quantified by pH scale (discussed in Lecture 13). «But reactivity of acid does not just depend on the amount of H⁺. Also depends on the anion.» Ex: HF(aq) → H⁺(aq) + F⁻(aq), hydrofluoric acid: «Weak acid but still nasty due to F⁻. Very reactive! Very corrosive!» *(hw)* Engineers commonly use HF to clean or etch glass or electronic surfaces (e.g. in ETH's clean room). Be careful! HF is «bone seeker». Exposure to skin: get help immediately!

**20 (p. 18) Neutralization Reactions.** *(hw)* (ℓ) = liquid; NaCl = table salt; but «salt» more generally means ionic compound for which cation comes from base and anion from acid. **Molecular equation:** HCl(aq) [acid] + NaOH(aq) [base] → H₂O(ℓ) + NaCl(aq) [salt]. **Complete ionic equation:** H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(ℓ) + Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). **Net ionic equation:** H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(ℓ). «Neutralization reaction: H⁺ and OH⁻ combine to form H₂O.»

**21 (p. 19) Cleaning Limescale (Kalk).** *(hw)* We use neutralization reactions to clean our bathrooms. Recall: bases do not need to contain OH⁻. Photo of a tap with limescale. «Scale from hard water is typically CaCO₃.» Scale forms due to … **Precipitation reaction:** Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s). **Neutralization reaction:** use vinegar (acetic acid) to clean: CaCO₃(s) + 2 CH₃COOH(aq) → H₂O(l) + Ca(CH₃COO)₂(aq) + CO₂(g); labelled: insoluble base, acetic acid, product of neutralization, calcium acetate (salt), carbon dioxide. *(hw)* CaCO₃ = scale deposits on shower walls and in tea kettles; acetic acid CH₃COOH → CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ (acetate ion); see bubbles when you clean away scale? Reaction is actually a little more complicated than this (more later) but interesting for us because we use it so much in Switzerland due to our «hard» water (full of dissolved minerals). Also, CaCO₃(s) used as «antacid» to neutralize excess stomach acid. The process creates CO₂ gas. Sold as «Rennie» in Switzerland.

**22 (p. 20) Oxidation–Reduction Reactions or «Redox» reactions.** *(hw)* So far today …: first, we introduced precipitation reactions: cation + anion → insoluble compound (cations and anions are exchanged between reactants); then acid/base reactions, where a proton is transferred between reactants. Now, a third class of reaction, where electron is transferred between reactants. Again, first look. More in Lecture 14. «Classic example: Corrosion ⇒ reaction between elemental metal and its environment.» Photos: rusty bolt, tarnished silver cup. Rust ⇒ Fe loses electrons to O₂ in air ⇒ Fe₂O₃ (*(hw)* and other iron oxides). Tarnish ⇒ Ag loses electrons to H₂S ⇒ Ag₂S. *(hw)* Redox reactions are super important for engineers to understand. Critical for batteries, corrosion, fuel cells, electroplating …

**23 (p. 21) Oxidation / Reduction.** *(hw)* Called oxidation because the first such reactions that were studied involved a metal that gave its electrons to oxygen, forming a metal oxide like in rust (Fe₂O₃). **Oxidation:** when an atom, ion, or molecule loses electrons — becomes more positively charged. Example: 2 Ca(s) + O₂(g) → 2 CaO(s); each calcium atom starts as neutral ⇒ Ca²⁺ (oxidized). **Reduction:** when an atom, ion, or molecule accepts electrons; each oxygen atom starts as neutral ⇒ O²⁻ (reduced).

**24 (p. 22) Oxidation and Reduction Go Together!** One species gives up electrons, other accepts. «To remember difference between oxidation and reduction ⇒ "LEO the lion says GER"»: **L**osing **e**lectrons is **o**xidation; **G**aining **e**lectrons is **r**eduction. «Book-keeping system to keep track of transferred electrons?»

**25 (p. 23) Oxidation Numbers or oxidation state.** *(hw)* Before proceeding, we need a book-keeping system to keep track of how many electrons are transferred. «Each atom in a neutral substance or ion assigned an oxidation number.» For monoatomic ions ⇒ oxidation number is same as its charge. For neutral molecules or polyatomic ions ⇒ oxidation number for each constituent atom is its hypothetical charge (*(hw)* we are artificially distributing electrons between all of the atoms in molecule). «Rules to assign oxidation numbers to each atom?» *(hw)* In this course, we will follow convention used in textbook: actual charges ⇒ number first and then the sign ⇒ 2⁺, 2⁻ (we also mentioned this in Lecture 1, p. 21); oxidation state ⇒ sign first and then the number ⇒ +2, −2. Ex: Ca²⁺ cation has +2 oxidation number.

**26 (p. 24) Oxidation Numbers — rules.** *(hw)* Note! In general, the oxidation number is not the same as electrical charge on atom. It just gives us a way to track electron flow in redox reactions. Rules: atoms in elemental form: 0. Monoatomic ions: ionic charge. Nonmetals in ionic/molecular compounds: negative oxidation numbers: oxygen: −2 (except peroxide ion, O₂²⁻, −1); H: +1 (except if bonded to metal, −1) *(hw: Ex: NaH, sodium hydride)*; F: −1 (always); Cl, Br, I: −1 (except positive if bonded to oxygen). Sum of oxidation numbers for atoms in compound equals its net charge *(hw: can use this rule to check your assigned oxidation number or assign one atom after easy ones are done)*. Ex: Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g) with oxidation numbers 0; +1, −1; +2, −1; 0. «Mg is oxidized; Mg reduces H; Mg is reductant *(hw: or reducing agent)*. H is reduced; H oxidizes Mg; H is oxidizer *(hw: or oxidizing agent)*.» *(hw)* These types of reactions between a metal and acid (or salt) known as «displacement reactions»: A + BX → AX + B.

**27 (p. 25) Activity Series.** *(hw)* How can we predict which metals will be oxidized? «Metals listed in terms of ease of oxidation.» Table (Brown): Lithium Li(s) → Li⁺(aq) + e⁻ — easiest to oxidize ↓ …; Iron Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻; …; Gold Au(s) → Au³⁺(aq) + 3e⁻ — hardest to oxidize. *(hw)* Noble metals are near bottom of activity series ⇒ not reactive! Makes sense! Gold not easy to corrode ⇒ used as stable currency. See Table 4.5 in Brown for complete list. Maybe not surprising that we use Li in lithium-ion rechargeable batteries (Lecture 14).

**28 (p. 26) Using Activity Series.** *(hw)* We can use activity series to predict if certain metal will react with another metal cation or acid. Excerpt of the table: Lithium, ⋮, Nickel Ni(s) → Ni²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻, ⋮, **Hydrogen H₂(g) → 2 H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻** (blue), ⋮, Copper Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻, Silver Ag(s) → Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ (arrow: harder to oxidize downwards). «Any metal can be oxidized by metal cations below it in series»: Cu(s) + 2 Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2 Ag(s) *(hw: Ag below Cu)*. «Any metal above hydrogen in series can react with acid to form H₂»: Ni(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → NiCl₂(aq) + H₂(g) *(hw: Ni above H₂)*. *(hw)* These questions of reactivity are important for electrochemistry. We will return to this topic in Lecture 14 on batteries. Ex: How can we manipulate the electron flow here for useful electrical power?

## Exercise class 3 (filled-in slides, PDF pages)

**p. 6 (3.6 Acid–Base Reaction ③).** acid–base reactions: an acid and base exchange an H⁺ ion. Acid: a molecule that ionizes in H₂O to form H⁺ — the reactant that donates = gives the proton; e.g. HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). Base: a molecule that accepts H⁺ — the reactant that accepts = takes the proton; either directly accepts an H⁺, e.g. NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) → NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq), or forms OH⁻ that then reacts with H⁺ to H₂O, e.g. NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). **Neutralization reaction:** special type, an acid reacts with a base and forms a salt and water. H₂SO₄ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaSO₄ + 2 H₂O (molecular equation; acid loses H⁺, base wins H⁺, salt, water); complete ionic: 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → 2 H₂O + Ca²⁺ + SO₄²⁻; net ionic: 2H⁺ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O [only ions and molecules that change].

**p. 7 Example 4.** «HCl and Al(OH)₃ react. Write down the proper molecular equation and the net ionic equation.» 3 HCl + Al(OH)₃ → 3 H₂O + AlCl₃ (ions: H⁺ Cl⁻; Al³⁺ OH⁻; Al³⁺ Cl⁻); net ionic as written by the TA: 3H⁺ + 3OH⁻ → 3 H₂O. (**Source problem CH-001:** Al(OH)₃ is insoluble by the chart of slide 16 (OH⁻ compounds are insoluble except those of NH₄⁺, the alkali metals, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺), so it would stay as a solid in the net ionic equation: Al(OH)₃(s) + 3H⁺(aq) → Al³⁺(aq) + 3 H₂O(l). Compare slide 21, where insoluble CaCO₃(s) stays a solid.)
**3.7 Redox Reaction ④:** oxidation–reduction (= redox) reaction: electrons transfer between atoms during reaction; «LEO the lion says GER» (lion drawing): oxidation: losing electrons; reduction: gaining electrons; oxidation numbers: to keep track of electron flows — the charge atoms would have if bonds were completely ionic; the rules box of slide 26.

**p. 8 Example 5.** «Put the oxidation numbers beneath each atom»: H₂O: +1, −2; O₂: 0; Na⁺: +1; MgCl₂: +2, −1; CO₂: +4, −2. (Meme: «Anything on the periodic table: give me the electron!» with fluorine.) **Example 6** (Brown): «Which element is oxidized and which is reduced in the following reactions? **p. 9 (3.8 Activity Series).** activity series: list of metals in order of ease of oxidation; the top metals are the easiest to oxidize, the bottom ones the hardest to oxidize; any metal can be oxidized by metal cations below it, e.g. Li + Na⁺ → Li⁺ + Na; any metal above hydrogen on the list can react with an acid to form H₂, e.g. magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid to form H₂: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g) with oxidation numbers 0, +1 −1, +2 −1, 0. **Table 4.5 Activity series of metals in aqueous solution** (full table, top = easiest to oxidize): Li → Li⁺ + e⁻; K → K⁺ + e⁻; Ba → Ba²⁺ + 2e⁻; Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻; Na → Na⁺ + e⁻; Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻; Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻; Mn → Mn²⁺ + 2e⁻; Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻; Cr → Cr³⁺ + 3e⁻; Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻; Co → Co²⁺ + 2e⁻; Ni → Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻; Sn → Sn²⁺ + 2e⁻; Pb → Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻; **H₂(g) → 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻**; Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻; Ag → Ag⁺ + e⁻; Hg(l) → Hg²⁺ + 2e⁻; Pt → Pt²⁺ + 2e⁻; Au → Au³⁺ + 3e⁻.
**Example 7** (Brown 4.19): «Which combination of metal and metal ion do you expect to undergo a redox reaction? (a) Ca(s) and K⁺(aq), (b) Fe(s) and Al³⁺(aq), (c) Ag(s) and Cu²⁺(aq), (d) Fe(s) and Pb²⁺(aq).» Answer: d) — only combination with metal above metal cation on list.
