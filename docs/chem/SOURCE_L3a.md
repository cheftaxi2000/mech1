## Lecture 3

**8 (p. 6) Reactions in Aqueous Solutions.** «Last lecture: gases. Today: liquids.» «What is a solution?» → «Homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.» **solution = solvent + solutes.** Solvent? Substance present in greatest amount. Solutes? Substances «dissolved» in solvent. Aqueous solutions? Solvent is H₂O. «H₂O is unusual in many ways (more later). Good at dissolving ionic solids (e.g. NaCl) …» *(hw)* Note: solvent can be a solid! Ex: steel involves C atoms (solute) dissolved in solid Fe (solvent). But today, solvent is a liquid ⇒ H₂O. *(hw bottom)* If it weren't, we would not have life on Earth!

**9 (p. 7) Electrolyte** — «Substance that dissolves in H₂O by forming ions.» Hand drawing: beaker with H₂O and a block of solid NaCl (red dots Na⁺, blue dots Cl⁻ in a lattice) → «Dissolves» → beaker with Na⁺ and Cl⁻ scattered. «Ionic solid dissociates into individual cations and anions.» *(hw)* Called electrolytic solution. Aqueous solutions of electrolytes conduct electricity; pure H₂O and aqueous solutions of nonelectrolytes do not. Important for batteries (Lecture 14).

**10 (p. 8) Why is H₂O Good Solvent for Ions?** Water molecule drawn bent: O with δ⁻ (blue), two H with δ⁺ (red). «Delta means partial charge. Partial means less than e. Oxygen has partial negative charge. Hydrogen has partial positive charge.» *(hw)* We will return to these ideas in more detail in the coming weeks. Drawings: Na⁺ (red dot) surrounded by four water molecules with the O (δ⁻) pointing to the ion; Cl⁻ (blue dot) surrounded by water molecules with the H (δ⁺) pointing to the ion. «H₂O orients itself to stabilize charge of ions.»

**11 (p. 9) Nonelectrolyte** — «Substance that dissolves in H₂O without forming ions.» Hand drawing: sugar cube (blue molecules in a lattice) in water → «Dissolves» → sucrose molecules scattered. «Nonelectrolytes dissolve when individual molecules dissolve in H₂O.» *(hw)* Another example of a nonelectrolytic solution: ethanol molecules (CH₃CH₂OH) dissolve as liquid solute in H₂O ⇒ beer. Sucrose = C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (structure drawing, wikipedia.org).

**12 (p. 10) Why Do Substances Dissolve in H₂O?** «Complicated question! Not all substances do.» Need thermodynamics to determine which dissolve (*starting next lecture*). Both energetic and entropic reasons for solute to dissolve: energetic = system (H₂O + solute) lowers its energy by dissolving; entropic = system (H₂O + solute) increases its disorder by dissolving. «For now: accept that some substances are soluble in H₂O and others not soluble.» *(hw)* «Soluble» means it dissolves; «insoluble» means it does not.

**13 (p. 11) Precipitation Reactions.** *(hw)* «Because some substances dissolve and others do not, this leads to class of reactions known as …» **Precipitate ≡ insoluble solid formed by reaction.** Ex 1: AgNO₃(aq) + KCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + KNO₃(aq). «aq» means aqueous, i.e. dissolved in H₂O; «s» denotes solid precipitate; AgCl(s) used in photographic film *(hw: AgCl can be converted to Ag(s) with exposure to light)*. Ex 2: 2 Na(C₁₇H₃₅COO)(aq) + CaCl₂(aq) → Ca(C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂(s) + 2 NaCl(aq) (soap = sodium stearate; «soap scum»; *(hw)* Ca²⁺ dissolved in «hard» water like in Switzerland). **Exchange or Metathesis Reactions:** AX + BY → AY + BX; in precipitation reactions cations (A, B) and anions (X, Y) trade partners. *(hw)* Precipitation reactions belong to a broader class known as … (→ acid/base, redox).

**14 (p. 12) Complete Ionic Equations versus Net Ionic Equations** (first build-up). «Above, reaction written as "molecular equation," with all ionic compounds as molecules»: AgNO₃(aq) + KCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + KNO₃(aq). «Other ways to write same reaction. Remember these reactants are ionic solids that dissolve …»

**15 (p. 13) same slide, completed.** **Complete Ionic Equation:** Ag⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) + K⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) + NO₃⁻(aq) + K⁺(aq) («Write out all dissolved ions»; «AgCl is not dissolved; it is precipitate»). Note: K⁺ and NO₃⁻ are on both sides of the equation. Called **spectator ions** *(hw: do not participate in reaction)*. **Net Ionic Equation:** cancel spectator ions from both sides of equation: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s). «How do we know if precipitate will form? Use solubility chart …» *(hw)* Rule of thumb: any substance with solubility below 0.01 mol/L is considered insoluble. We will also return to solubility in Lecture 9.

**16 (p. 14) Solubility for Common Ionic Compounds in H₂O** (Table 4.1 in Brown, *(hw)*).
*Soluble ionic compounds* — compounds containing: NO₃⁻ — important exceptions: none; CH₃COO⁻ — none; Cl⁻ — compounds of Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺, and Pb²⁺; Br⁻ — compounds of Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺, and Pb²⁺; I⁻ — compounds of Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺, and Pb²⁺; SO₄²⁻ — compounds of Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺, Hg₂²⁺, and Pb²⁺.
*Insoluble ionic compounds* — compounds containing: S²⁻ — important exceptions: compounds of NH₄⁺, the alkali metal cations, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, and Ba²⁺; CO₃²⁻ — compounds of NH₄⁺ and the alkali metal cations; PO₄³⁻ — compounds of NH₄⁺ and the alkali metal cations; OH⁻ — compounds of NH₄⁺, the alkali metal cations, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, and Ba²⁺.

**29 (p. 27) Concentration.** *(hw)* «Finally, we need language to quantify amount of aqueous solute …» *(hw)* In general, we can express concentration of solute in a solution using … **Molarity ≡ moles of solute / volume of solution in liters.** Example: 1 molar solution ⇒ 1 mol / 1 L ≡ 1 M. Note: this is not the only way to express concentration (*(hw)* more in Lecture 9). Bullets: need to be able to convert between M, V, and n (*(hw)* molarity, volume, and moles); common class of problems on exam; get practice on PS #3. *(hw bottom)* «NEXT TIME: 1ˢᵗ LAW OF THERMODYNAMICS».

## Exercise class 3 (filled-in slides, PDF pages)

**p. 2 (3.2 Solutions).** solution = solvent + solute; solvent: present in greatest amount; aqueous solution: solvent is water; **electrolytes**: type of solute that dissolves in water to ions (picture of an NaCl crystal dissolving; «the partial charges on oxygen and hydrogen orient to stabilize cations and anions»; drawing: Na⁺ near the O (δ⁻) of waters, Cl⁻ near the H; e.g. NaCl, HCl, KOH); **nonelectrolytes**: solute dissolves in water as individual molecules (e.g. sugar, ethanol, sucrose); **electrolytic solution**: electrolytes dissolved in water → conducts electricity through ions; pure water and nonelectrolytic solutions do not conduct; **molarity**: quantifying concentrations: molarity = moles of solute / volume of solution = [mol/L] = [M].

**p. 3 Example 1.** «What is the concentration of ethanol in human blood in units of molarity if the blood alcohol concentration, BAC, is 0.08? BAC has units of g ethanol / 100 mL blood.» M(ethanol) = 46 g/mol. c = n/V = (0.08 g / 100 mL) · (1 mol / 46 g) · (1000 mL / 1 L) = 0.02 mol/L (as written; 0.0174 unrounded).
**3.3 Reactions in water:** transfer ions → metathesis reactions ① and precipitation reactions ②; transfer protons → acid–base reactions ③; transfer electrons → oxidation–reduction reactions ④. **3.4 Metathesis reaction ①:** exchange/metathesis reaction: reactants exchange partners; AB + CD → AD + CB.

**p. 4 (3.5 Precipitation Reaction ②).** precipitation reaction: two ionic solids dissolve and exchange partners; one new pair is an insoluble solid, a precipitate, that has been formed. AgNO₃(aq) + KCl(aq) → AgCl(s) (boxed «precipitate») + KNO₃(aq) with cation/anion marked + and −, KNO₃ circled «spectator ions»; drawing of a beaker with a layer of precipitate at the bottom. How to solve these tasks: note the chemical reaction and ions present; consider combinations (+ to −); check solubility chart to see if products are soluble [insoluble if they attach to each other strongly]. (solubility table as on slide 16.)

**p. 5 Example 2.** «Using solubility guidelines, predict whether each of the following compounds is soluble or insoluble in water: (a) Hg₂SO₄, (b) NH₄OH, (c) Ni(CH₃COO)₂, (d) AgNO₃, (e) FeCO₃.» Answers: a) no, b) yes, c) yes, d) yes, e) no. **Example 3:** «What happens if we mix Mg(NO₃)₂ and NaOH in water?» Mg(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2 NaOH(aq) → Mg(OH)₂(s) (boxed) + 2 NaNO₃(aq), ions marked 2+, 2−; «Mg(OH)₂ precipitates to an insoluble solid.»
