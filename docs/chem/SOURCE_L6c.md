Slide numbers = PDF pages (header «Lecture #6, p. k», k = slide − 1).

### Slide 28 (p. 27) — Screening
- Question: «For a given shell (n), why is E_s < E_p < E_d < E_f ?»
- Figure: «multi electron atom»: nucleus with Z protons (red dot), core electrons (black rings round it), valence electrons (blue rings further out).
- Text: Valence electrons repelled by core electrons. Valence electrons don't feel full attraction from +Z (handwritten: «From protons in nucleus»). Core electrons «screen» nucleus. Valence electrons feel Z_eff < Z (handwritten: «Z_eff ≡ effective nuclear charge»). Effect is stronger for subshells further from nucleus (handwritten: «Explains E_s < E_p < E_d < E_f»). Average distance for electrons increases for s → p → d → f. Also, Z_eff increases across a row in the periodic table.
- Handwritten bottom: «Number of core electrons stays the same, but Z is increasing as we move right across a period in the periodic table. In principle, Z_eff should stay approximately the same as we move down a column in the periodic table. However, Z_eff increases slightly because electron cloud becomes more diffuse ⇒ less effective screen.»

### Slide 29 (p. 28) — Atomic Size
- Handwritten top: «It is also useful to have measure of size of atoms and ions …» «A little "fuzzy" because of electron cloud».
- «Different ways to define …» Typical: bonding atomic radius ≡ d/2 for bond length d (figure: two atoms with nucleus and electron cloud, bond length d). Table of atomic radii ⇒ estimate new bond lengths.
- Trends: Atomic radii increase down group ⇒ n (shell) is getting larger (handwritten: «Down column in periodic table»). Atomic radii decrease across period ⇒ Z_eff is increasing (handwritten: «Left to right across row in periodic table»; «So valence electrons get pulled in by larger positive Z_eff»).

### Slide 31 (p. 30) — Ionic Radii
- Handwritten top: «Similarly, we can estimate sizes of ions …»
- «Estimate ion sizes from bond lengths in ionic solids.» Cations smaller than neutral parent ion. Anions larger than neutral parent ion. Figures: Na atom (11p⁺, 11e⁻) loses an electron → Na cation (11p⁺, 10e⁻; 1+); Cl atom (17p⁺, 17e⁻) gains an electron → Cl anion (17p⁺, 18e⁻; 1−).
- Trends: Ions with same charge ⇒ bigger down group (handwritten: «Down group i.e. as we go down in periodic table»). Ions with same number of electrons ⇒ smaller with increasing Z (handwritten: «i.e., as nuclear charge increases, the electrons are held more tightly»). Ex: isoelectronic series O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ (all have 10 electrons), r_ionic = 126, 119, 116, 86, 68 pm; arrow «smaller».

### Slide 32 (p. 31) — Ionization Energy, Electron Affinity
- Handwritten top: «Finally, we need to define two useful parameters …»
- **Ionization Energy**: energy needed to ionize (remove electron from) atom or ion. Ex: Na → Na⁺ + 1 e⁻ (I₁); Na⁺ → Na²⁺ + 1 e⁻ (I₂). Handwritten: «More accurately, the minimum energy needed», «First and second ionization energies». Trends: I₂ > I₁. IE increases left to right across a period. IE decreases down a group of the periodic table. Handwritten bottom: «Note: Ionization energy, I > 0 → We have to add energy to remove an electron.»
- **Electron Affinity**: energy change when we add extra electron to an atom. Ex: Cl + 1 e⁻ → Cl⁻ (EA). Process is typically (but not always) exothermic ⇒ EA < 0. Trends: Halogens ⇒ EA ≪ 0; Noble gases ⇒ EA > 0 (handwritten: «Makes sense considering full shell»). Handwritten bottom: «But EA < 0 (in convention we are using) ⇒ system lowers its energy by accepting another electron and making anion. Different convention: EA defined as "energy released" by atom ⇒ EA is then given as positive number.»
- No numerical values of I or EA on the slide.
